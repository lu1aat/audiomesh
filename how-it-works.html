audiomesh · How it works

A network made of sound

audiomesh sends text between browsers through nothing but a speaker and a microphone. No Wi-Fi, no server, no cable: the air in the room is the network. This page follows one message from the keyboard to another device's screen. The demos run audiomesh's own modulator, decoder and network code, right here in the page.

Chapter 01

The air is the cable

A phone's speaker and microphone cover a wide range of pitches. audiomesh splits that range into three bands and uses one at a time: low (100–300 Hz), audible (300 Hz–10 kHz) and ultrasonic (17.5–21 kHz). Ultrasonic is the default. It carries well across a room and most adults can't hear it.

Each band holds fixed, numbered channels, the same on every device. A station listens to every channel of its band at once, so a frame can go out on any of them and still be heard.

Chapter 02

Text becomes bits

Every frame carries exactly 77 bits. To fit more text in them, audiomesh uses a 6-bit alphabet of 64 symbols: capital letters, digits and punctuation. Lower case arrives as upper case, accented letters without their accents (É as E, Ñ as N) and anything else as a question mark, the same trade FT8 and JS8 make.

The first frame of a message says who sent it, who it is for and which message it belongs to, which leaves room for 7 characters. Each following frame carries 9 more. A message can use up to 16 frames, which makes 142 characters.

In the code: src/chat/frames.ts and src/chat/charset6.ts. Station ids are random numbers from 1 to 1023; names travel in separate “hello” frames.

Chapter 03

Armour against noise

Sound in a room fades, echoes and collides with other sound. So the 77 bits get two layers of protection before they go out. A 14-bit CRC lets the receiver check its own work. Then an LDPC code (the one FT8 uses) adds 83 parity bits, for a 174-bit codeword. Almost half of what goes on the air is redundancy that lets the receiver rebuild what it lost.

The 174 bits become 58 symbols of 3 bits each. Below is the first frame of your message, symbol by symbol. Damage it and watch the real decoder try to recover it.

A symbol the receiver knows it missed costs far less than one it heard wrong. The decoder survives about 20 erased symbols out of 58, but only about 6 wrong ones. Either way it never returns wrong text. When the parity checks or the CRC fail, it returns nothing.

In the code: src/protocol/gfsk8/codec.ts, ldpc.ts (belief propagation, up to 30 iterations) and crc14.ts. The LDPC tables come from ft8_lib, which takes them from WSJT-X.

Chapter 04

Bits become tones

Three bits choose one of eight tones, so each symbol is a short whistle at one of eight pitches. The tones sit exactly one symbol rate apart (6.25 Hz in Normal), which is the closest spacing at which a receiver can still tell them apart.

Neighbouring tones differ in just one bit, a Gray code. The most common mistake, energy landing one tone off, then costs one bit instead of up to three.

The pitch also glides from tone to tone along a Gaussian curve instead of jumping. A jump would splatter energy into neighbouring channels. The glide keeps the whole frame within the width of 8 tones.

The frame as tones Gfsk8Modulator

Yellow symbols are the sync pattern, teal ones carry your data. Playback runs at 1 kHz so you can hear it; the app sends the same thing in the band you choose.

In the code: src/protocol/gfsk8/modulator.ts. Bandwidth-time product 2.0, the FT8 choice. Samples are made on the fly, a block at a time, so the audio thread never waits.

Chapter 05

Finding the frame

The receiver doesn't know exactly when a frame starts or at exactly what pitch. Clocks disagree a little, and sound cards run a few hundred parts per million apart. So every frame carries the same 7-tone pattern three times, at its start, middle and end: 3 1 4 0 6 5 2. It is a Costas array, a pattern that lines up with a shifted copy of itself in only one place.

The receiver slides the pattern over the whole recording and asks, at every position: what share of the energy in those 21 symbols lands on the predicted tones? Pure noise scores about 0.23. A real frame scores 0.3 or more, usually far more.

This picture uses a coarse grid: whole tones and quarter symbols. The real receiver steps by 1/32 of a symbol and about 1 Hz, then hands its 8 best candidates to the decoder.

In the code: src/protocol/gfsk8/demodulator.ts. It mixes the channel down to 0 Hz, keeps 32 samples per symbol and measures the tones with a sliding DFT.

Chapter 06

Everyone keeps time

Stations never agree on when to talk. Time is already cut into slots counted from 1 January 1970, UTC: 15 seconds each in Normal. Any device with a correct clock knows when every slot starts without asking anyone. A station starts sending at a slot boundary.

The receiver records a bit more than one frame, from a little before the boundary to a little after the frame should end. A frame from a clock that is somewhat fast or slow still fits inside. The Sync tools in the app help when it doesn't.

Chapter 07

The receiver, end to end

Now all of it at once. Choose how weak the signal is and how far off the other station's clock and sound card are, then send a frame. The page builds what a microphone would hear (the real modulator plus white noise) and gives it to the real receiver. It mixes the channel down, measures the eight tones, searches for the sync, decodes the best candidates and checks the CRC.

Signal strength is measured against the noise in 2500 Hz, the weak-signal radio convention. At −18 dB the noise in that width is about 60 times stronger than the signal and you can't hear the frame at all. In Normal it still decodes.

The lab runs at 1 kHz with an 8 kHz sample rate to stay light. The app does the same at 17.5–21 kHz and 48 kHz, on every channel of the band, every slot.

In the code: src/dsp/decoder.worker.ts and src/protocol/gfsk8/demodulator.ts. When the plain decode fails but the sync looks real, the receiver tries harder: other ways of reading the tones, and bits it can predict, like the shape of an ack it is waiting for.

Chapter 08

Did it arrive?

A message to one station gets an answer. Once the receiver has heard the last frame, it sends an ack: a 16-bit map with one bit for each frame it holds. The sender then resends only the frames the map lacks, for up to four rounds. A message to everyone is never acked.

A station that transmits can't hear, so the sender sends nothing new while it waits. And since decoding takes a moment after each slot, an answer always goes out two slots after the frame it answers.

In the code: src/chat/session.ts. A complete ack goes out twice, two slots apart, because a lost ack would otherwise cost a whole resend round.

Chapter 09

Choosing a channel

There is no control channel. Everyone decodes the whole band, so the question is which channel the other station hears best. Speakers, microphones and rooms differ, and how well you hear them says little about how well they hear you. So stations tell each other: every ack and every beacon carries reports like “I hear #101 on channel 12 at −9 dB”.

To send, a station takes the worst case over everyone it is sending to, avoids channels where others have been transmitting (each one costs 4 dB), keeps the channels within 3 dB of the best and picks one of them at random, so that not everyone piles onto the same channel.

Tap a channel's Busy cell to add other transmitters there (up to two).

In the code: src/ale/lqa.ts and src/ale/channel-load.ts. ALE (automatic link establishment) is the HF radio idea behind it.

Chapter 10

One hop further

When two stations can't hear each other, one in between can relay. A repeater sends again what it hears directly from others, two or four slots later, with a 3-bit tag that says who repeated it. It never repeats a repeat, so a frame travels one hop at most. It never repeats beacons either, since a beacon measures one particular link.

In the code: src/chat/repeater.ts. The tag is the repeater's id modulo 7, plus 1: three bits are all the room a frame has left.

Chapter 11

Speed or reach

Every mode uses the same frame, the same code and the same sync. Only the symbol rate changes, and everything else follows from it. Halve the rate and frames take twice as long, reach about 3 dB deeper into the noise and carry half the text per minute. Both stations must use the same mode.

Six modes tap one to use it on this page

Sensitivity was measured in simulation with white noise, at 48 kHz, with unknown timing; never a wrong payload. Turbo, Fast and Normal have also been used over the air. Two more variants, Spread and Burst, trade speed for tolerance of wobbling or fading signals.

Try it with two devices

Open the app on two phones or laptops in the same room. Pick the same mode and band on both, turn on Audio and Allow transmit, and send a message. Check that both clocks are right first.

Open audiomesh

audiomesh runs entirely in the browser: no backend, and no audio ever leaves the device. It borrows from FT8 and JS8 but does not talk to them. The full over-the-air specification is network-protocol.md in the repository.
